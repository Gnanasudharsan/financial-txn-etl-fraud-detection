## 1. Fraud model (LightGBM)

Time-based split: train on Jan–Apr 2026 (first 14 days excluded as feature burn-in), tune the
threshold on May to reach ≥ 95% recall, evaluate once on June.

### Interpretation

- **The pipeline and evaluation are sound; the problem is easy.** At the validation-tuned
  threshold the model catches 95.17% of June fraud while 99.28% of its alerts are real fraud,
  alerting on 0.75% of transactions. Real card fraud rarely looks this clean: published
  benchmarks on real data typically report PR-AUC far below 0.99. Here every fraud pattern is
  generated by a handful of rules, so a gradient-boosted model can learn them almost exactly.
- **Simulation v1 was even easier, and was fixed rather than reported.** The first run had
  PR-AUC 0.9994 with 12 false positives in June
  ([`model_metrics_sim_v1.json`](results/model_metrics_sim_v1.json)). After ruling out leakage,
  legitimate look-alikes were added (VPN geo-jumps, domestic fraud proxies, hijacked known
  devices, chip-cloned bursts; see D-011). PR-AUC fell to 0.9974 and false positives roughly
  quadrupled (44). That is the expected direction, and it shows the evaluation is sensitive to
  the data rather than hard-wired.
- **Where it misses.** Capture rate on June fraud by pattern: velocity bursts 90.0%
  (1,352 / 1,502), high-amount-at-night 92.7% (596 / 643), account takeover 93.0% (962 / 1,034),
  high-risk MCC 97.5% (1,068 / 1,095), card testing 99.5% (2,055 / 2,065). Velocity bursts on
  a chip or contactless entry in a nearby city, and night purchases by customers who shop late,
  are the patterns that overlap most with legitimate behavior.
- **Feature reliance is concentrated.** `implied_speed_kmh` still carries 50.9% of the gain,
  `mcc_risk_score` 19.7%, and `amount_usd` 17.9%. On real data this concentration would be a
  robustness risk worth monitoring (feature drift, travel seasonality).
- **No leakage by construction.** Strict time split, burn-in excluded, all windows end before
  the transaction, MCC risk fitted on the training window only, merchant fraud rates using only
  reports dated before the transaction day. The threshold was chosen on May and applied
  unchanged to June; validation and test metrics agree closely (precision 99.11% vs 99.28%).

Of the 4,914,701 ledger entries, 2 were classified differently from the injected truth. Both
were injected as `amount_mismatch`, but the random 2–50% perturbation of a very small amount
produced a difference within the $0.05 tolerance, so the engine called them `tolerance_match`.
Under the stated rules that is correct, and it is an artifact of the injector. All 2,494
orphan settlements and 4,913 duplicates were found exactly.

### Automation benchmark (manual baseline is an ESTIMATE)

**Measured:** reconciling all 181 business days (4,914,701 ledger + 4,902,456 settlement rows),
publishing three gold tables, and writing the CSV break report took **67.4 s**
(66.0 s match + publish).

**Estimated manual baseline.** These are assumptions, not measurements: a spreadsheet-based
daily recon for one business day (~27k ledger rows) by an experienced analyst.

| # | Manual step | Est. minutes / business day |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pull the ledger extract and the processor settlement file | 10 |
| 2 | Normalize dates, amounts, reference formats in a spreadsheet | 30 |
| 3 | Lookup-match on reference; flag amount and date differences | 45 |
| 4 | Chase settlements without a reference by auth code + merchant | 60 |
| 5 | Classify breaks, apply tolerances, review duplicates | 45 |
| 6 | Build and circulate the break report and summary | 30 |
| | **Total** | **220** |

**Reduction, using that estimate:**
- Per business day: if each daily run took the full measured 67.4 s (it would take less),
  then 1 − 67.4 s / 220 min = **99.49%** less time.
- Whole period: 220 min × 181 days ≈ 664 analyst-hours vs
  67.4 s of compute = **99.997%** reduction.

The per-day figure is the defensible one to quote. Both depend entirely on the assumed manual
minutes above.
